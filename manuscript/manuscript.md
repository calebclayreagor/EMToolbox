**Results**  
**Developmental scRNA-seq data for comparison of e-EMT programs**

We first sought to perform an unbiased, cross-lineage comparison of the transcriptional events encompassing e-EMT by collecting published scRNA-seq data of mouse embryogenesis from our group and others. Although EMT reshapes diverse tissues across development including visceral organs such as the liver, stomach, lungs, and heart, we reasoned that these systems constituted noisier examples of shared e-EMT programs due to the presence of orthogonal transcriptional processes, including terminal lineage commitment and asynchronous EMT-MET cycles [(Thiery *et al.*, 2009; Han *et al.*, 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FfJvar). We therefore selected three examples of primary e-EMT—namely, gastrulation [(Scheibner *et al.*, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KXKUHk), posterior somitogenesis [(Jin *et al.*, 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?F01GWt), and neural crest migration [(Poverennaya *et al.*, 2026\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?bMcomk)—to facilitate our comparison of the shared transcriptional programs driving EMT across early-to-mid embryogenesis (**Figure 1a**; **Supplementary Table 1**).  
	These datasets comprised several progenitor-cell populations that experienced unique cell-fate bifurcations between mesenchymal and other lineages. During gastrulation (E6.5-7.5), pluripotent epiblast cells underwent a lineage bifurcation between lateral plate mesoderm (LPM) and definitive endoderm (DE; Figure 1a, left) [(Scheibner *et al.*, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?eK1AzZ), whereas later epiblast-derived neuromesodermal progenitors (NMPs; E8.5) underwent a bifurcation between the presomitic mesoderm (PSM) and neural tube (Figure 1a, middle) [(Jin *et al.*, 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NoqkBM). In the developing head (E8.5-10.5), migratory neural crest cells (NCCs) also underwent a bifurcation between mesenchymal and neuroglial fates (Figure 1a, right) [(Poverennaya *et al.*, 2026\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?gFEWdY). Together, these datasets presented a detailed description of primary e-EMT across multiple tissues and stages and therefore constituted an important resource for uncovering the shared transcriptional programs driving e-EMT during development.

**Shared transcriptional programs comprise e-EMT across cell types**

To identify shared transcriptional signatures across our selected datasets, we first subset all cell types that contributed to mesenchymal lineages across the observed bifurcations (Figure 1a, dashed boxes). These cells displayed strong expression of several core EMT-TFs including *Snai1*, *Twist1*, and *Zeb2* (**Figure 1b**) [(Yang *et al.*, 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hwavGv), with all three datasets exhibiting similar cross-lineage expression patterns between progenitors and mesenchymal populations. Notably, the mesenchymal lineages in gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis displayed nearly identical patterns of EMT-TF expression including early upregulation of *Snai1* and *Zeb2* and later onset of *Twist1*, *Foxc1/2*, and *Prrx2*. However, these cell type-specific expression patterns only provided a coarse readout of the shared transcriptional programs orchestrating e-EMT across developmental lineages.  
	We next employed our group’s preexisting computational framework scFates to perform pseudotime analysis across the mesenchymal lineages and identify genes whose expression significantly varied along these trajectories (**Figures 1c, d**) [(Faure *et al.*, 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8QJ0dL). Each trajectory comprised a single cell-state gradient originating in pluripotent or multipotent progenitors and terminating in fate-restricted mesenchymal cells along the lineage. Subsequent statistical modeling identified 219, 217, and 215 genes whose expression was significantly associated with the inferred pseudotime trajectories for gastrulation (Figure 1d, left), posterior somitogenesis (Figure 1d, middle), and neural crest migration (Figure 1d, right), respectively. This analysis uncovered many expected early- and late-stage markers for gastrulation—such as *Epcam* (early) and *T*, *Mesp1*, and *Fn1* (late)—and posterior somitogenesis, including *Cdx2*/*4* (early) and *Tbx6*, *Foxc2*, and *Fn1* (late) (CITATIONS). To identify shared transcriptional programs across trajectories, we next implemented a deep-conservation filter to select 79, 98, and 104 genes, respectively, that possessed at least one homolog in the genomes of both *Homo sapiens* and *Drosophila melanogaster*, reasoning that these genes might also possess conserved functions across developmental lineages [(Gabaldón and Koonin, 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KFHdNE) (**Supplementary Table 2**). We subsequently refer to these gene modules as Gast, pSom, and Crest.  
	Although their genes were expressed at various pseudotimes along each trajectory, these modules’ relative expression signatures—defined as a module’s mean expression relative to the mean expression of the other two modules—formed a similar progression across the three trajectories (Figure 1d, bottom panels). In gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis, pSom expression (early) preceded Gast expression (mid) and finally Crest expression (late), while both Gast and pSom expression (early) preceded Crest expression (late) in neural crest migration. The enrichment of each signature along its respective trajectory also corresponded with the expression of temporally segregated submodules (Figure 1d, vertical lines), suggesting that distinct transcriptional phases were more prominent within certain trajectories. However, these results also demonstrated that shared transcriptional programs comprised e-EMT across divergent lineages from both ancient germ layers such as the mesoderm and newer, vertebrate-specific innovations such as NMPs and NCCs [(Sauka-Spengler *et al.*, 2007; Sambasivan and Steventon, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fP1zPZ).

**Distinct e-EMT modules encompass shared signaling pathways**

To further elucidate the functional relationships between shared e-EMT transcriptional programs, we next compared the membership of individual genes across Gast, pSom, and Crest (**Figure 1e**). Although these modules were associated with similar morphogenetic processes, their specific gene-level compositions were largely dissimilar, with Gast and pSom displaying the strongest pairwise similarity (Jaccard index \= 0.18). However, because pseudotime analysis and downstream feature selection can be sensitive to trajectory resolution and model selection, we also performed gene ontology (GO) term-enrichment analysis to identify the top biological processes associated with each module (**Figure 1f; Supplementary Tables 3-5**) [(The Gene Ontology Consortium *et al.*, 2026\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?C7WzN3). While Gast and pSom both exhibited strong enrichment for GO terms related to transcriptional regulation, lineage commitment, and Wnt signaling, Crest was enriched for terms related to RNA processing and ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex assembly, which was consistent with our prior observation that ribosomal modifications are associated with mesenchymal fate selection in NCCs [(Poverennaya *et al.*, 2026\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SqHol3).  
	We also used STRING to analyze the functional enrichment of each module’s protein-protein interaction network [(Szklarczyk *et al.*, 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Js2wRs), which revealed a striking pathway-level convergence: all three modules contained clusters for Wnt signaling—a canonical EMT-activating, upstream pathway—while pSom and Crest both contained clusters for retinoic acid (RA) signaling, and Gast contained clusters for TGF-β and Notch signaling (**Figure 1g; Supplementary Figures 1-3**) [(Youssef and Nieto, 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?oMIsFd). These results suggested that while Gast and pSom were somewhat similar on the level of individual genes, pSom and Crest were more similar at the signaling pathway level, despite notable differences in their genes’ biological processes.

**MesenCoder integrates mesenchymal trajectories across posterior epiblast lineages**

Motivated by our observation of shared, cross-lineage e-EMT signatures and signaling pathways, we next developed a suite of computational tools called EMToolbox to elucidate the relationships between mesenchymal genes, modules, programs, and cell states in development and cancer. To understand if these modules encoded emergent properties such as shared cell-state manifolds, we first constructed a conditional autoencoder called MesenCoder that used the gene expression values from Gast, pSom, and Crest to generate integrated, low-dimensional embeddings across e-EMT trajectories (**Figure 2a**). Using these simplified representations, our model then employed self-supervised learning to reconstruct the observed expression values of the 237 genes comprising the union of the e-EMT modules. Though similar to approaches such as scVI [(Lopez *et al.*, 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DaFA2c), our algorithm simplified the neural-network architecture to comprise shallow encoder/decoder networks with only one hidden layer each, as well as a deterministic 2D latent space designed to encourage interpretable, semantically rich cell-state representations.  
	Because their trajectories displayed the strongest concordance across e-EMT signatures, we selected the mesenchymal lineages from gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis to train and validate MesenCoder, with each trajectory consisting of an 80/20 data split. After gradient descent-based optimization, our autoencoder produced an integrated 2D embedding across the trajectories, with one dimension, MesenCoder1 (MC1), encoding a split between the posterior epiblast lineages, and the second dimension, MesenCoder2 (MC2), encoding a shared progression along each lineage (**Figure 2b**). Although these trajectories occupied distinct regions of the integrated latent space, the embeddings abutted a common boundary (Figure 2b, dashed line), which suggested these lineages represented a shared transcriptional manifold.

**Early cross-repression of e-EMT modules facilitates a cell-state bifurcation**

To determine if our integrated, low-dimensional embeddings of the mesenchymal trajectories from gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis constituted a shared transcriptional manifold, we next used scFates to fit a principal tree to the cross-lineage 2D latent representation (**Figure 2c**) [(Faure *et al.*, 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8WncAm). The topology of the inferred tree suggested that these trajectories form a transcriptional bifurcation, with epiblast cells selected as the root, posterior epiblast cells occupying the branch point, and LPM and PSM comprising the tips. Notably, these relationships recapitulated the known developmental hierarchy between posterior epiblast cells and NMPs—which together straddled the cross-dataset boundary—and therefore might indicate a role for e-EMT modules in mesenchymal lineage commitment [(Henrique *et al.*, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YsPDn0).  
	We next sought to disentangle the relationships between e-EMT modules and the inferred bifurcation by visualizing the modules’ signatures across the integrated embeddings, which revealed a branch-specific enrichment of Gast and pSom across the LPM- and PSM-terminal arms, respectively (**Figure 2d**). This observation provided further evidence that—despite their utilization across all three trajectories—these e-EMT modules were more prominent within specific trajectories and might also facilitate the transcriptional divergence underlying the inferred bifurcation through mutually repressive regulatory interactions [(Soldatov *et al.*, 2019\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UWReiW). To test this hypothesis, we computed the correlation of Gast and pSom signatures with the expression of each gene that was an exclusive member of one but not both of these modules (*i.e.*, XOR), which we then used to compute the cross-module correlation along the gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis trajectories (**Figure 2e**). This analysis revealed that Gast-pSom mutual repression peaked before the inferred branch point, suggesting that these modules primed the epiblast cells for selection between LPM- or PSM-terminal branches prior to their transcriptional divergence.  
	To further investigate the gene-module relationships across the bifurcation, we also subset the epiblast cells and NMPs from the gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis trajectories, respectively (Figure 2e, dashed boxes). The cross-module correlations for the genes in either Gast or pSom (XOR) were strongly negative in the early epiblast cells (*r* \= \-0.47; *t* \< *t*median\_Epi for trajectory pseudotime *t*), near zero in the late epiblast cells (*r* \= \-0.09; *t* \> *t*median\_Epi), and strongly positive in the NMPs (*r* \= 0.59) (**Figure 2f**). The observed shift from negative to positive cross-module correlations surrounding the branch point suggested that Gast-pSom interactions switched from mutual repression (*i.e.*, pre-branch) to co-activation (*i.e.*, post-branch) by E8.5 in the elongating posterior embryo.  
	Several developmental regulators from both modules (*i.e.*, Gast∩pSom) displayed strong positive correlations with both Gast and pSom signatures across the epiblast cells and NMPs—such as *T* (early epiblast; NMP), *Msx1* and *Sp5* (late epiblast; NMP), and *Msx2* and *Id1*/*3* (early/late epiblast; NMP). However, the anterior mesendodermal TF and Gast gene *Eomes* displayed strong polarization in the late epiblast cells (*r*Gast \= 0.10; *r*pSom \= \-0.40), suggesting that LPM vs. PSM lineage commitment occurred after the strongest Gast-pSom cross-repression [(Arnold *et al.*, 2008\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?06R48F). Notably, the RA-signaling gene *Crabp2* represented the strongest polarized pSom gene in the early epiblast cells (*r*Gast \= \-0.09; *r*pSom \= 0.31), consistent with the enrichment of RA-signaling genes in pSom and a potential role for this pathway upstream of the bifurcation [(De Angelis *et al.*, 2026\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZDsc0m). Collectively, these results supported a model where e-EMT modules facilitated a cell-state bifurcation that preceded LPM vs. PSM lineage commitment, indicating that cell state and fate were uncoupled across the embedding [(Weinreb *et al.*, 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Pt6may).

**MesenCoder infers alternative cell states and programs for full and partial e-EMT**

Although our cross-module correlation analysis suggested that the bifurcation represented divergent transcriptional states that were separate from their developmental lineages, these results could not alone indicate the biological identities of the inferred states. We therefore employed the third, unseen e-EMT trajectory for neural crest migration as a held-out testing dataset to assess MesenCoder’s embedding of a transcriptionally similar but lineage-independent cell-state transition (**Figure 2g**). Strikingly, our algorithm projected the unseen, neural crest trajectory exclusively onto the PSM-terminal branch, indicating that this branch corresponded to partial e-EMT states, while the alternative, LPM-terminal branch corresponded to full e-EMT states. We subsequently refer to these branches’ programs as embryonic full EMT (e-fEMT) and embryonic partial EMT (e-pEMT), defined by the relative enrichment of Gast vs. pSom and/or Crest signatures, respectively. This result also supported our hypothesis that RA signaling acted upstream of the e-pEMT program because both pSom and Crest—but not Gast—contained RA-signaling clusters in their STRING networks.  
	We lastly sought to understand the potential relationships between these alternative e-EMT programs and several core EMT-TFs by visualizing their genes’ expression domains across each trajectory’s embedding (**Figure 2h**). These results demonstrated that—in addition to late *Twist1* expression and mid-to-late *Snai1* expression across all three trajectories—both the posterior somitogenesis and neural crest migration trajectories displayed early *Zeb1* expression, suggesting a role for this TF upstream of the e-pEMT program. Moreover, the mean gene expression magnitude of the 237 genes comprising MesenCoder’s input features was tightly correlated between both gastrulation and posterior somitogenesis (*r* \= 0.74) and posterior somitogenesis and neural crest migration (*r* \= 0.74), but was weaker between gastrulation and neural crest migration (*r* \= 0.64) (**Supplementary Figure 4**). Two of the three genes whose expression was the highest across the neural crest trajectory relative to the gastrulation trajectory were *Crabp1*/*2*, which were also enriched in the posterior somitogenesis trajectory relative to the gastrulation trajectory. Together, these results validated the learned and inferred co-occupancy of the partial e-EMT branch by the posterior somitogenesis and neural crest migration trajectories, respectively, as well as the likely upstream involvement of RA signaling.
